MCP Under Neutral Foundation Governance: What Enterprise Adopters Should Ask For

MCP foundation governance lowers lock-in risk but does not end it. Check release cadence, deprecation policy and who signs off on breaking changes.

In my work with platform teams, the first question after “should we adopt MCP?” is almost always “who owns it?” It is a fair question. Nobody wants to wire forty internal systems to a protocol and then find out the owner changed the rules, or the pricing, or lost interest.

The ownership answer changed when Anthropic contributed the Model Context Protocol to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund hosted by the Linux Foundation. The Linux Foundation’s announcement named MCP, Block’s goose and OpenAI’s AGENTS.md as the founding projects, with Amazon Web Services, Anthropic, Block, Bloomberg, Cloudflare, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI as platinum members. The project itself is established as “Model Context Protocol a Series of LF Projects, LLC” under the governance page, and the trademarks sit under LF Projects policy rather than with one lab.

That is good news, and I will say why. But I have watched teams treat “neutral foundation” as a synonym for “safe”, and it is not. A foundation answers who owns the name and the legal wrapper. It does not, by itself, tell you how often the spec will break your integration, how long you get to migrate, or who can overrule the people you are talking to. Those are the questions that decide your engineering budget.

Key Takeaways
  • Foundation hosting lowers ownership and trademark risk. It does not set release cadence, deprecation windows or breaking-change authority, and those are what drive your maintenance cost.
  • Read three documents before committing: the versioning page, the feature lifecycle and deprecation policy, and the governance page that names who holds final say.
  • Neutral governance is one input. A proprietary protocol can still be the better fit when you need one vendor's roadmap, a support contract or a faster-moving feature set.
  • Budget for a spec upgrade at least twice a year and put a contract test suite between your agents and every tool server.

What the foundation actually changes

Be precise about the gain. Three things moved when MCP went to a neutral home.

First, ownership of the brand and the legal entity. Governance changes need approval from LF Projects, LLC as well as from the maintainers. The governance document says contributions are made under the Apache License 2.0, and that contributors keep copyright rather than assigning it. A single lab can no longer rename the protocol or relicense it on its own.

Second, no member can buy a seat. The technical governance document says membership of the maintainer groups belongs to individuals, not companies, and that no seats are reserved for specific companies. Platinum members fund the foundation. They do not appoint the people who decide what goes into the spec. I would not oversell that: the maintainers still have employers, and employers have interests. But the structure is built to separate money from technical decisions.

Third, process became written down. Spec changes go through Specification Enhancement Proposals, with recorded votes, and the lifecycle for retiring features is a published policy rather than a habit.

What did not change is the protocol, the SDKs, or the people doing most of the work. If the maintainers you trust are still there next year, your experience will be similar. If they are not, the process is what protects you. That is why the process is worth reading closely.

The three questions that matter

1. How often does the spec move, and does the date hold?

A foundation that ships whenever it feels ready gives you no planning horizon. MCP’s process gives you one. A process proposal, SEP-3398, sets specification releases every six months, generally on the last Tuesday of March and September, with release managers confirming exact dates in each cycle’s published calendar. Each release runs on an eight-month cycle: two months of planning, a Core Maintainer review window, then stabilization. The proposal says scope slips, not the date. Unfinished work waits for a later release.

The milestones are expressed relative to release day. Scope freezes 2.5 months before release, Tier 1 SDK betas arrive 1.5 months before, and a hard freeze one month before restricts changes to critical security fixes and release-blocking defects. Treat those as planning targets: the proposal says the published release calendar holds the exact deadlines.

For an adopter this is useful in a concrete way. You can put “evaluate new spec revision” on the calendar, and you know roughly when SDK betas will let you test. The honest caveat is that a written cadence is a promise, not a track record. The proposal itself calls for a retrospective after the first release run under it, and it describes a transition calendar for adopting the schedule. Check the live release calendar and ask to see how the latest cycle went, rather than trusting the table.

Also read how versions are named. The versioning page says MCP versions are date strings that mark the last date a backwards incompatible change was made, and that the version is not incremented for backwards compatible updates. So a new date means something broke for somebody. Treat each new date as a migration ticket until you have read the changelog and learned otherwise.

2. How long do you get to migrate?

The feature lifecycle and deprecation policy is the document I would hand to a procurement or architecture review board. A feature is Active, Deprecated or Removed. Deprecation requires a SEP that documents a migration path and sets a minimum window of at least twelve months, which the versioning page repeats. Removal is only eligible in the first Current revision released after that window ends. Tier 1 SDKs must mark the deprecated surface using the language’s native mechanism, and should emit a runtime warning when it is used.

Two details deserve attention. The clock starts when the revision carrying the deprecation is released as Current, not when the proposal is approved. And there is an expedited exception: for an active security risk, meaning a published advisory or documented in-the-wild exploitation with no in-place mitigation, the window can shrink, but it still must provide at least ninety days.

So the worst case written into policy is ninety days, and only for security. The normal case is a year or more. For a regulated enterprise with change-freeze windows and a quarterly release train, that is the difference between a manageable upgrade and an incident. Write the ninety-day case into your own runbooks anyway.

One more nuance. The policy governs spec features. It says removal from the spec does not oblige an SDK to drop the feature, because SDKs follow their own revision-support policy. Your real exposure is the SDK you depend on, so read the SDK tiering and support policy for it as well as the protocol documents.

3. Who signs off on a breaking change?

This is the question people skip, and it is the one that tests the word “neutral”. The governance page describes four tiers: contributors, maintainers, Core Maintainers and Lead Maintainers. Core Maintainers steer the specification, vote at a regular meeting, and can veto maintainer decisions by majority. Under the release process in SEP-3398, acceptance of a spec change needs affirmative votes from more than half of all eligible Core Maintainers, not merely a majority of those who attended, and the vote stays open for seven days.

Above them sit the Lead Maintainers, described in the document as the final decision makers, or BDFL, with the power to veto any decision by Core Maintainers. They also appoint and remove Core Maintainers. The governance page names the people who hold that role, so check it directly rather than relying on this article. They are expected to publicly articulate their reasoning.

I am not criticising this. The governance page describes the structure as similar to Python, PyTorch and other open source projects, and a clear tiebreaker is often better than a committee that cannot decide. But name it for what it is. “Vendor-neutral” describes the legal home and the absence of company seats. It does not mean the decision-making is distributed, and the effective control over breaking changes rests with a small, named group of individuals. Your risk is concentration and continuity, not a hidden corporate agenda. Ask what the succession rules are. The governance page leads to a separate amendment and succession proposal, SEP-2085, which is the place to look.

The diagram shows where an adopter can intervene. You have no vote, but you have two places to act: before the freeze, by commenting on proposals and the roadmap, and after the SDK beta, by running your own tests against it. Most enterprises do neither and learn about changes from a failed deployment.

A worked example: the handshake change

This is not hypothetical. Version negotiation has changed between spec revisions. Earlier revisions used an initialization handshake. In the newer model every request declares its protocol version, the server accepts or rejects each request independently, and a discovery call returns the versions a server supports. The versioning page keeps a backward-compatibility section for the handshake-based revisions, and says clients and servers may support several versions at once. Read that page to see which revision applies to you.

For a team running a handful of servers it is a day or two of work. For a team running a gateway in front of dozens of internal tool servers, owned by different squads, it is a program. Which servers pin the old version? Which clients are bundled inside an IDE plugin you do not control? Who tells the squad that owns the payments tool server that its SDK needs upgrading?

The foundation did nothing wrong here. The change went through a public process, the compatibility path is documented, and multi-version support is allowed. But the lock-in you feel in that situation is not to a vendor. It is to a moving specification, and no governance model removes that. If you want a technical treatment of keeping tool contracts stable, I covered tool schema versioning and contract testing separately. Here the point is organisational: someone must own the upgrade calendar.

When a proprietary protocol is still the better fit

I would be giving you a one-sided picture if I stopped there. Neutral governance buys durability and multi-vendor support. It costs you speed and a single accountable owner. There are situations where a proprietary or vendor-specific tool-calling interface wins.

  • You are committing to one platform anyway. If your agents run on one provider’s runtime and you have a support contract with that provider, their native tool interface may be better integrated, better documented and covered by an SLA. A foundation process gives you no one to call at two in the morning.
  • You need a feature the spec does not have yet. A foundation moves on a published cycle. A vendor can ship a capability next week. If that capability is core to your product, waiting for a standards cycle is a real cost.
  • Your surface is small and stable. Three internal tools behind one model provider do not need a protocol layer. The adapter you would write in a day may be cheaper than the upgrade discipline a shared standard asks of you.
  • Your regulator wants a single accountable counterparty. Some compliance regimes are easier to satisfy with a contract naming a responsible vendor than with a community process.

On the other side, a multi-model estate, many teams and a long horizon favour the shared standard, because the cost of N bespoke adapters grows with every model you add. Many teams end up with both: MCP at the edge for shared tools, native interfaces inside the one platform where depth matters. This is a workload and team-maturity decision, not an ideology.

What to ask for, in practice

Here is the checklist I give architecture review boards. None of it needs special access. Every item is answerable from public documents or from your own vendors.

  1. Release calendar. What is the next scheduled spec release, when is scope frozen, and when do Tier 1 SDK betas land? Put those dates in your own roadmap.
  2. Deprecation register. Is anything you use listed in the deprecated features registry? What is its earliest removal date? Subscribe to changes to that page.
  3. SDK policy. Which SDK tier does your language have, and how long does it support old spec revisions? The SDK tiering policy is the place to look. Your exposure follows the SDK, not the spec.
  4. Decision rights. Who are the Core and Lead Maintainers, what happens if one leaves, and how are governance amendments approved? Check that the answer survives the loss of any one person.
  5. Your own contract tests. Run them against each beta. Pin the protocol version in production and upgrade deliberately, so a spec revision never reaches users by accident.
  6. Vendor commitments. If you buy MCP-compatible products, ask the vendor which spec revisions they support, for how long, and what they will do when one is removed.
  7. An internal owner. One named person or team owns the MCP upgrade calendar. Without that, every squad discovers the change separately.

The executive summary

If you are a leader who will not read the spec, here is the version to carry into the meeting. Moving MCP to a neutral foundation made the standard a safer bet by taking ownership out of one company’s hands. It did not make the standard free. You still pay for upgrades at least twice a year, you still depend on a small group of maintainers for breaking-change decisions, and you still need engineering discipline on your side of the wire.

The cost is predictable if you plan for it: an owner, a calendar, a contract test suite, and a pinned version. The cost is a surprise if you do not. Lock-in risk has moved from “who owns the protocol” to “how well do we manage change”, and that second one is a problem you control. Treat governance as one input next to capability, support, and the shape of your own estate, and revisit the decision when the facts change.
